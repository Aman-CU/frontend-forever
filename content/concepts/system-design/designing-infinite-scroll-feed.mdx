## The pagination contract: cursor, not offset

`GET /feed?after=<cursor>&limit=20` — the cursor is an opaque server-generated token (commonly an encoded `(timestamp, id)` pair) representing "everything after this specific item." Because it's tied to a row's identity rather than its numeric position, inserting new items above it never shifts what the next page returns — unlike `GET /feed?offset=20&limit=20`, where a single new insert above position 20 duplicates or skips an item across the boundary.

## The caching contract: don't refetch on back-navigation

Every page fetched so far should be cached client-side (a library like React Query/SWR does this by request key) so that navigating away and pressing back restores the feed instantly from cache rather than re-fetching from page 1. Without this, infinite scroll effectively punishes the browser's back button — exactly the navigation a feed relies on most.

## The scroll-position contract

Restoring from cache is only half the fix — the scroll position itself has to be remembered too (commonly in `sessionStorage`, keyed by route), and restored *after* the cached items have rendered, not before — restoring scroll position against an empty or partially-rendered list just lands somewhere wrong.

## Freshness without disrupting the reading position

New items appearing at the top while someone is mid-scroll, read is one of infinite scroll's worst UX failures — the content under their eyes visually shifts. The fix is to poll for new items at a low frequency without inserting them automatically; surface a small "X new posts" banner instead, and only insert on an explicit tap, so the feed's current, already-being-read content never moves out from under the user.

## Why it matters

"How would you design an infinite-scroll feed?" tests whether pagination, caching, and scroll position get treated as one connected contract or three unrelated afterthoughts — a correct cursor-based API paired with no client-side cache still refetches everything and loses scroll position on back-navigation, so all three pieces have to hold at once for the feature to actually feel seamless.
