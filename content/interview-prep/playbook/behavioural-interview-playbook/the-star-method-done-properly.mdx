Almost every candidate has heard of STAR. Very few use it well, because knowing the acronym isn't the same as knowing how to weight it. The most common failure mode isn't forgetting a step — it's spending 70% of the answer on Situation and Task, then rushing Action and Result into a single vague sentence at the end, right when the answer should be getting more specific, not less.

## Situation — brief, just enough context

One to two sentences. "Our team was three weeks from a launch when our design lead left unexpectedly, and I was the only frontend engineer with context on the design system." That's enough — the interviewer needs context to understand the stakes, not a full narrative of the quarter. If Situation is taking more than 15–20 seconds to say out loud, it's too long.

## Task — your specific responsibility, not the team's

This is the part candidates most often skip or blur into Situation. Be explicit about what *you* were actually responsible for, distinct from what the team as a whole was doing. "I needed to make sure the component library stayed consistent through the transition, and that the two engineers who joined mid-project had enough documentation to work independently." A vague Task makes it impossible to tell later whether the Result was actually yours.

## Action — roughly 60% of your answer

This is where most of your airtime should go, and it's where most candidates under-deliver. Put a real number on it: aim for **Action at roughly 60% of your total answer, with Situation and Task combined under 20%** (Result and the optional reflection beat below take up the rest). That's a testable target you can time yourself against, not just a vague "most of it." Weak: "I worked with the team to fix the issue." Strong: "I wrote a short onboarding doc for the two new engineers covering our component conventions, set up a 30-minute pairing session with each of them in their first week, and flagged inconsistencies I found during code review instead of fixing them silently, so the patterns actually stuck." Specificity is the entire value of Action — generic verbs like "helped," "worked on," and "was involved in" tell an interviewer nothing they can evaluate. Use "I," not "we," throughout, even inside a team story — the interviewer is scoring your specific contribution, and habitual "we" language is the single most common way a strong Action gets diluted into vague, unscoreable credit-sharing.

## Result — a real outcome, ideally with a number

"It went well" is not a Result. "We shipped on time, and the two new engineers were both independently contributing components within their second week instead of the four weeks it had taken the previous new hire" is a Result — concrete, comparable, and specific enough to be believable. Not every story has a clean quantitative metric, and that's fine — but every Result should be specific enough that a skeptical interviewer would find it hard to argue with.

## The bonus fifth beat: reflection

The strongest answers add a short closing line about what you learned or would do differently — "next time I'd write that onboarding doc before the crunch started, not during it." This signals growth-mindedness without you having to claim the trait directly, and it's a natural, low-cost way to preempt the inevitable "what would you do differently" follow-up.

## A quick self-check before an interview

For each story in your bank, time yourself telling it out loud and check it against the 60/20 split above. If Situation and Task together take longer than Action, rebalance. If Result is a single vague sentence, you're not done preparing that story yet — go back and find the actual number, or the actual concrete change, even if it takes some real thought to reconstruct it accurately.

STAR isn't a script to recite word-for-word — a story that sounds memorized instead of remembered is its own red flag. It's a weighting discipline: brief setup, specific action, concrete result. Once that weighting is automatic, you can adapt any real story to almost any prompt an interviewer throws at you.
