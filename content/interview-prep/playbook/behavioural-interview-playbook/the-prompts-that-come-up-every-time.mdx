Behavioral questions feel infinite in the moment, but they draw from a surprisingly small pool of underlying themes, worded differently by different interviewers. Recognizing the family a question belongs to — even when the exact wording is unfamiliar — lets you route to a prepared story instead of improvising from scratch.

## Conflict and disagreement

*"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate or manager." "Describe a conflict you had at work."*

Interviewers are checking whether you can disagree productively — stating a position clearly, engaging with the other side's reasoning, and reaching a resolution (even if the resolution was "I was wrong") without the relationship or the work suffering. The weakest answers here either avoid ever having a real disagreement ("I try to always find common ground" — evasive, not an actual story) or describe a conflict where you were simply right and the other person simply wrong, with no real tension in the account.

## Failure and mistakes

*"Tell me about a time you failed." "Describe a mistake you made and what you learned."*

This is the prompt candidates dread most and prepare for least, which is exactly why it's worth deliberate prep. The instinct to pick a "safe" failure (something that wasn't really your fault, or was so minor it barely counts) undermines the whole point — interviewers want to see real ownership and real change afterward, not a story engineered to make you look blameless. Pick something that actually stung, and be honest about what you'd do differently.

## Leadership without formal authority

*"Tell me about a time you led a project without being the manager." "Describe a time you influenced a decision you didn't own."*

Frontend engineers are asked this constantly even at non-management levels, because most real influence at a company happens without a title behind it — driving a technical decision, mentoring a newer engineer, pushing a process change. The strongest answers show specific persuasion or coordination tactics, not just "I was the most senior person in the room so people listened to me."

## Ambiguity and unclear requirements

*"Tell me about a time you had to make a decision without complete information." "Describe a project where the requirements changed partway through."*

This tests judgment under uncertainty — did you make a reasonable call and communicate it clearly, or freeze waiting for someone else to resolve the ambiguity? A good answer names the specific tradeoff you weighed and why you chose the direction you did, even though a different reasonable person might have chosen differently.

## Prioritization and tradeoffs under pressure

*"Tell me about a time you had too much to do and not enough time." "How do you decide what to cut when a deadline is at risk?"*

Interviewers are checking for a real decision-making process, not just "I worked longer hours." A strong answer names the specific criteria you used to prioritize (user impact, risk, reversibility) and what you explicitly chose to deprioritize and why — showing the tradeoff itself, not just that you survived it.

## Why-this-company and career motivation

*"Why are you interested in this role?" "Where do you want your career to go?"*

Not a story-based prompt, but worth preparing with the same rigor — a generic answer here ("I've always been interested in your mission") is as damaging as a vague STAR answer elsewhere. Be specific about what actually drew you to this particular team or product, grounded in something real about your own trajectory.

## The 6 families, at a glance

| Family | Example prompt | What's tested |
|---|---|---|
| Conflict and disagreement | "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate." | Disagreeing productively without the relationship or work suffering |
| Failure and mistakes | "Tell me about a time you failed." | Real ownership and real change afterward, not a blameless near-miss |
| Leadership without authority | "Describe a time you influenced a decision you didn't own." | Specific persuasion or coordination tactics, not just seniority |
| Ambiguity | "Tell me about a decision you made without complete information." | Judgment under uncertainty, communicated clearly |
| Prioritization under pressure | "How do you decide what to cut when a deadline is at risk?" | A real decision-making process, not just working longer hours |
| Motivation | "Why are you interested in this role?" | Specificity about this team or product, not a generic mission statement |

## Using this list

Map each of your prepared stories (see [Building Your Personal Story Bank](/interview-prep/playbook/behavioural-interview-playbook/building-your-personal-story-bank)) against these six families before an interview — if any family has no story attached, that's a specific, fixable gap worth closing before you walk in, rather than discovering it live when the exact prompt catches you flat-footed.
