Not every project you've built belongs on a resume, and including too many actively dilutes the strongest ones. The goal of a projects section is depth signal, not a complete inventory of everything you've ever shipped.

## The selection bar

For each candidate project, ask: does this demonstrate a specific, hard, defensible engineering decision — or does it just demonstrate that you can follow a tutorial? [Portfolio Projects That Land a React Offer](/interview-prep/playbook/react-interview-playbook/portfolio-projects-that-land-a-react-offer) covers what makes a project genuinely strong in the first place; this chapter is about which of your strong projects actually earn a spot on the resume itself, and how to describe them once they do.

## Two to three, not five or six

A resume with six shallow project bullets reads as less impressive than one with two deep ones, because a reader's attention is finite and a long list signals breadth-over-depth by its very shape. Pick your two or three strongest, cut the rest — even genuinely good projects, if you have more than three worth mentioning, are better moved to your GitHub profile itself (see [Cleaning Up Your GitHub and Portfolio](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/cleaning-up-your-github-and-portfolio)) than crammed onto the resume.

## Describing a project in the same impact-driven language as work experience

Apply the same formula from [Writing Impact-Driven Bullet Points](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/writing-impact-driven-bullet-points): name the specific hard problem, the approach, and a concrete outcome or metric where you have one. "Built a virtualized data table handling 50,000+ rows at 60fps, using windowing to keep DOM node count under 100 regardless of dataset size" is far stronger than "Built a data table component using React."

## When a personal or Build tab project belongs on a resume

If your work experience doesn't showcase the specific depth you want a resume to demonstrate — maybe your day job is mostly incremental feature work, or you're earlier-career with a thin work history — a well-executed personal project, including a Frontend Forever [Build tab](/learn) project you genuinely extended beyond the starter brief, is legitimate resume material. What matters is the same bar as any other project: does it demonstrate real engineering judgment, not just that a feature technically works.

## Always link to something live

A project without a working, clickable deployed link gets dramatically less attention than one a recruiter can open in a new tab during their initial scan (see [What Recruiters Scan For in 8 Seconds](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/what-recruiters-scan-for-in-eight-seconds)). If a project genuinely can't be deployed live for some reason, a short screen-recorded demo is a reasonable substitute — a GitHub link alone, with no way to see it working, is the weakest option available and should be a last resort.

## Rotating projects over time

Revisit your project selection every time you update your resume for a new search — a project that felt impressive two years ago may no longer represent your current skill level as well as something more recent does, even if the older one is objectively more polished. Recency and relevance to the specific role you're applying for both matter more than raw impressiveness in isolation.
