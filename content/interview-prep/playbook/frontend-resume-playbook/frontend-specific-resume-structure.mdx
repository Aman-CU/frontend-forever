Generic engineering-resume advice mostly transfers to frontend roles, but a few structural choices are worth handling deliberately, because frontend work has properties (visual, demo-able, framework-specific) that a one-size-fits-all resume template doesn't account for.

## The sections that matter most, in order

**Header.** Name, current title, contact info, and — uniquely valuable for frontend roles — links to a live portfolio and GitHub, not just LinkedIn. A recruiter who can click through to a working deployed project in seconds gets a stronger signal than any bullet point can provide alone.

**Skills / stack line.** A single scannable line or short list near the top — "React, TypeScript, Next.js, Node.js" — not a padded wall of every technology you've ever touched once. Precision here matters more than breadth; a recruiter matching against a specific job requirement is scanning for exact terms, and burying them in a list of 40 loosely-related keywords dilutes the ones that actually matter.

**Experience, reverse-chronological.** Standard structure, but each bullet should be impact-led (see [Writing Impact-Driven Bullet Points](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/writing-impact-driven-bullet-points)) rather than a responsibilities list. "Built and maintained the checkout flow" describes a duty; "Rebuilt the checkout flow's form validation, cutting cart-abandonment-on-error by 18%" describes an outcome — the second is what actually differentiates candidates at a glance.

**Projects.** Frontend is unusually well-suited to a dedicated projects section, since the work is visual and directly demo-able in a way a lot of backend or infrastructure work isn't. Two to three strong, deployed, linkable projects with a one-line description of the hard problem each one solved outperform a long list of tutorial-shaped clones — see [Which Projects to Feature](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/which-projects-to-feature).

**Education, kept brief** unless you're early-career, in which case it moves higher and can carry more weight (relevant coursework, notable academic projects).

## Length and formatting discipline

One page for anyone under roughly 8–10 years of experience; two pages only past that, and even then, trimmed ruthlessly. Consistent formatting (font, spacing, bullet style) across every section matters more than it seems — inconsistency reads as carelessness in a document whose entire job is to demonstrate attention to detail. Avoid dense paragraphs entirely; every substantive line should be a scannable bullet.

## ATS-safe formatting

Frontend engineers are the candidates most tempted to build a visually distinctive resume — and that instinct actively works against a resume being read at all, since a large share of applications are parsed by ATS software before a human sees them (see [Tailoring Your Resume Per Company, Fast](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/tailoring-your-resume-per-company-fast) for why this matters mechanically, not just aesthetically). Keep the underlying document single-column with standard section headings ("Experience," "Projects," "Skills") rather than a multi-column or graphic-heavy layout — parsers frequently misread multi-column text as out of order, or skip content inside tables and text boxes entirely. Keep contact info as plain text, not an icon-and-graphic header block, since icons commonly aren't parsed as text at all. Export to PDF from a text-based source (a document editor or a well-structured HTML-to-PDF export) rather than a flattened image or a heavily templated design tool export, so the underlying text stays selectable and machine-readable.

## What to deliberately leave off

Skip an "Objective" statement — it wastes prime top-of-resume real estate on a generic sentence a recruiter already assumes ("seeking a challenging frontend role..."). Skip listing every technology you've used for a single afternoon; a padded skills list is easy for an experienced reader to detect and actually undercuts credibility on the technologies you do know well. Skip a References section — "available on request" is universally assumed and doesn't need its own line.

## Tailoring the structure per application, not just the wording

The section order and relative emphasis above is a strong default, but see [Tailoring Your Resume Per Company Fast](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/tailoring-your-resume-per-company-fast) for how to adjust which specific bullets and projects get surfaced first depending on what a given role's description actually emphasizes — the structure stays consistent; the content underneath it shouldn't be identical for every application.
