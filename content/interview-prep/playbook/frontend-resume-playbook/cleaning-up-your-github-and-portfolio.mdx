A resume's job is to earn a click. What happens after that click — on your GitHub profile, your portfolio site — either confirms or undermines the impression the resume just built. A strong resume followed by a cluttered, unmaintained GitHub profile is a genuinely common and avoidable way to lose momentum right when a reader was most engaged.

## Pin your strongest repositories

GitHub lets you pin up to six repositories to the top of your profile — use this deliberately. A profile with fifty repositories and no pins forces a reader to guess which ones matter, and most won't bother digging. Pin your 3–6 strongest, matching the same selection bar from [Which Projects to Feature](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/which-projects-to-feature) — genuinely hard problems solved well, not just the most recent commits.

## Write a real README for every pinned project

A pinned repository with no README, or a default one-liner, wastes the attention it just earned. A strong README includes: what the project does and why you built it, a link to the live deployed version, a screenshot or short GIF if the project is visual, the specific technical challenge you're proud of solving, and basic setup instructions if someone wanted to run it locally. This is one of the highest-leverage 20-minute investments available before a job search, and it's routinely skipped.

## Archive or remove abandoned clutter

Old tutorial clones, half-finished experiments, and course-assignment repositories with generic names all dilute the signal of your genuinely strong work when a reader scrolls past your pins to your full repository list. You don't need to delete your history, but archiving clearly-abandoned or purely-educational repos (GitHub's archive feature keeps them without cluttering your active profile) keeps the overall impression focused on what best represents your current level.

## Keep commit history honest, not performative

Padding commit history with meaningless commits purely to look active is usually easy to spot and can read as more concerning than a quieter, honest history — reviewers who look closely care about the quality and clarity of commits on your featured projects far more than raw commit frequency across your whole profile.

## Your portfolio site itself

If you have a personal portfolio site beyond GitHub, apply the same 8-second-scan discipline from [What Recruiters Scan For in 8 Seconds](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/what-recruiters-scan-for-in-eight-seconds): lead with your strongest 2–3 projects above the fold, make sure every "live demo" link actually works (a broken link on a frontend engineer's own portfolio is a uniquely bad look), and keep the site itself fast and clean — a slow or visually cluttered portfolio undercuts your credibility on the exact skills the job posting is evaluating.

## A pre-application checklist

Before sending out applications in earnest: pin your best 3–6 repos, write or update each pinned repo's README, archive obvious clutter, click every link on your resume and portfolio to confirm it still works, and do the 8-second scan test on your portfolio site the same way you'd test your resume. None of this takes long, and all of it directly protects the impression your resume just worked to create.
