The oft-cited "recruiters spend 6-8 seconds on a resume" is real, and it changes how a resume needs to be written — not shorter for its own sake, but structured so the handful of things that matter most are impossible to miss even in a fast scan.

## What actually gets looked at first

**Current or most recent title and company.** This anchors your level and trajectory instantly — a recruiter scanning for a Senior Frontend Engineer role forms an immediate impression from "Senior Frontend Engineer at X" that a buried or vaguely-titled equivalent doesn't provide.

**Recognizable technology keywords.** React, TypeScript, Next.js — whatever stack the role calls for, in a scannable location (a skills line near the top, or visible in your most recent role's description), not only buried in project descriptions three pages in.

**A number or concrete outcome near the top.** "Reduced page load time by 40%" or "shipped a design system used across 12 product teams" registers in a fast scan in a way generic phrasing like "worked on frontend improvements" never will. See [Writing Impact-Driven Bullet Points](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/writing-impact-driven-bullet-points) for how to construct these properly.

**Overall visual scannability.** Dense paragraphs, inconsistent formatting, or a cluttered layout actively work against you here — a recruiter's eye needs to land on the right information without effort, and a resume that demands close reading just to locate basic facts loses attention before the actual content gets evaluated at all.

## What this means structurally

Assume the first pass is almost entirely visual pattern-matching, not close reading — that means: don't bury your strongest project or outcome deep in a wall of text, don't rely on the reader to infer your seniority from context clues, and don't make recognizable keywords hard to spot. [Frontend-Specific Resume Structure](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-resume-playbook/frontend-specific-resume-structure) covers the concrete layout this implies.

## The follow-up read, if you survive the scan

If the 8-second scan is favorable, most recruiters or hiring managers give a resume a second, closer pass — this is where project depth, specific technical decisions, and career trajectory actually get evaluated. The first pass' job is only to earn that second pass; it's not where nuance or depth gets rewarded, so don't spend your top-of-resume real estate on subtlety the fast scan can't register.

## A useful test

Cover your resume with a piece of paper and reveal it for 6 seconds, then cover it again. Write down everything you remember. If that list doesn't include your current level, your core stack, and at least one concrete outcome, your resume's structure — not necessarily your actual experience — is the thing holding you back, and that's a fixable, mechanical problem rather than a reflection of your real qualifications.
