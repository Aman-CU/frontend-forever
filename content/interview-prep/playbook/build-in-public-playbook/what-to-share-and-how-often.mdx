The biggest reason people quit building in public within a few weeks isn't lack of discipline — it's not knowing what to post on a Tuesday when nothing dramatic happened. A repeatable content loop solves that: it means every ordinary week has something worth sharing, not just the milestone weeks.

## A repeatable loop, not a blank page every time

Three moves, in rotation: a **progress update** ("shipped X this week," ideally with a screen recording or screenshot — visual, working progress is consistently the highest-engagement content type), a **decision and why** (a real tradeoff you made and the reasoning behind it — "chose cursor-based pagination over offset because the feed needs to handle concurrent inserts"), and a **lesson learned** (a bug that took longer than expected, a wrong assumption you corrected). Cycling through these three means you're never staring at a blank post wondering what's interesting enough to share — ordinary week-to-week engineering work already contains all three.

## The 80/20 rule

Roughly 80% of what you share should be genuinely useful or informative on its own — even to someone who never clicks through to whatever you're building — and about 20% can be direct promotion (a launch announcement, a "check this out" post). Content that's constantly asking for attention without offering anything in return trains an audience to scroll past it; content that's useful first earns the attention that makes the occasional promotional post actually land.

## Don't lead with metrics you don't have yet

A lot of "build in public" advice is written for founders sharing revenue and user-growth numbers — that's a different game with different stakes, and it's not the right template for an engineer building a career, not a company. Sharing a fabricated or inflated milestone to keep up with that format is worse than sharing nothing; specific, honest technical content (what you built, why, what broke) is both more credible and more useful to the audience that actually matters here — other engineers, and the people who might hire you.

## A sustainable cadence beats a heroic one

Consistency matters more than frequency. A realistic, sustainable target is one to three posts a week, chosen deliberately rather than forced daily — daily posting is what most people who quit building in public were attempting when they quit. If you can only sustain one solid post a week, one solid post a week for a year outperforms four posts a week for three weeks followed by silence, both for the compounding effect described in [Why Building in Public Compounds Your Career](/interview-prep/playbook/build-in-public-playbook/why-building-in-public-compounds-your-career) and for your own ability to keep doing it without it becoming a second job.

## Where to actually post

Pick one primary place and go deep rather than spreading thin across five platforms from week one: a personal blog or dev.to for longer technical writeups (these are also indexable, so they keep surfacing in search long after you've stopped thinking about them), X or LinkedIn for shorter progress updates and the professional-network effect, and your project's own README or changelog for anything tied directly to a specific piece of work. Adding a second platform once the first one has a real rhythm is far easier than trying to maintain three from the start.

## The through-line

Every piece of this loop — progress, decision, lesson — is content you'd have anyway just by doing real engineering work. Building in public isn't a separate project bolted onto your actual work; it's a habit of narrating the work you're already doing, in public, instead of only in a private commit message.
