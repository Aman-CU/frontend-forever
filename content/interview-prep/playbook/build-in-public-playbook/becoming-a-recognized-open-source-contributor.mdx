A first merged PR feels like the milestone, but it's really just the entry point. The engineers who get real recognition — direct outreach from companies, invitations onto a project's core team, a maintainer role — almost never got there through a wide spread of one-off contributions across many projects. They got there by returning to the same project, repeatedly, until they understood it more deeply than someone drive-by ever could.

## Depth on one project beats breadth across many

Contributing regularly to a single project compounds in a way scattered contributions don't: you start recognizing patterns in the codebase, understanding *why* certain design decisions were made (not just what they are), and building enough context to spot bugs and opportunities other contributors would miss entirely. This is also exactly the kind of context a résumé bullet can't fake — see [Turning Public Work Into Career Leverage](/interview-prep/playbook/build-in-public-playbook/turning-public-work-into-career-leverage) for how that shows up in an interview.

## What "recognized" actually looks like before maintainership

Recognition rarely arrives as a title first. It shows up as smaller signals: a maintainer starts tagging you on issues in your area of the codebase, your PRs get reviewed faster because your track record is already trusted, you're asked to weigh in on a design discussion instead of just implementing someone else's spec, or you start helping newer contributors get unblocked the way someone once helped you. Each of these is worth noticing and leaning into — they're the actual path, not a side effect of it.

## How projects find their next maintainers

Well-run projects don't run an application process for new maintainers — several actively track their most consistent, highest-quality contributors and reach out directly, asking if they'd want a more active role. This means the reliable way to be considered isn't asking for the role; it's being visibly, consistently useful over time until the project notices on its own. Becoming a maintainer is less about how many hours you can suddenly commit and more about being willing to commit to the project for the long haul.

## What actually changes once you're a maintainer

The role adds responsibilities beyond writing code: reviewing other people's pull requests thoughtfully (not just approving, but teaching), triaging and labeling incoming issues, making project-direction calls, and — repeatedly cited as one of the most underrated maintainer skills — writing things down. Documentation isn't just a courtesy to users; it clarifies your own thinking about the project and multiplies your usefulness beyond whatever hours you personally have available. No maintainer of a large project understands the entire codebase alone — the role is coordination and judgment as much as it is code.

## A realistic, low-pressure path

Pick one project you already use or find genuinely interesting. Make a few small contributions. If it feels good, keep going — a second PR, then engaging in an issue discussion instead of just fixing bugs, then reviewing someone else's PR if the project allows it. There's no fixed timeline here and no obligation to end up as a maintainer at all — plenty of engineers get real career value from being a known, trusted regular contributor without ever taking on the maintainer role, and that's a completely legitimate place to stop.
