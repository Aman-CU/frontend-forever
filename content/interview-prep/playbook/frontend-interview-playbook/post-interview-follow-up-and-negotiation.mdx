What happens after the last round is often treated as an afterthought, but two things here — how you follow up, and how you negotiate an offer — have real, measurable impact on the outcome.

## Following up without being pushy

A short thank-you note within a day of your final round is a small, genuinely useful gesture if you have contact info — it's not going to save a weak interview, but it costs nothing and occasionally surfaces something worth mentioning that didn't come up live. Keep it to a few sentences.

Ask the recruiter directly, before you leave the last call, what the expected timeline is — "when should I expect to hear back?" This isn't presumptuous, it's standard, and it tells you when a follow-up email is reasonable versus premature. If that date passes with no update, one polite check-in is appropriate; repeated daily follow-ups are not.

## Reading a delay correctly

A slow hiring-committee process at a large company routinely takes one to two weeks after your last interview — that's normal and not a signal either way. What is worth noting: if a recruiter goes fully unresponsive well past the timeline they gave you, that's more often a company-side process problem than a reflection on your interview. It's reasonable to keep interviewing elsewhere in parallel the entire time; treat every process as non-exclusive until you have a written offer.

## When the offer comes: always negotiate

Most companies build negotiation room into an initial offer, expecting a counter — accepting the first number left on the table is close to leaving free money behind in a large fraction of cases. Negotiating respectfully essentially never rescinds an offer at a company operating in good faith; it is a normal, expected part of the process.

**Before you respond to any number:**
- Get the full offer in writing — base, bonus, equity (and its vesting schedule), sign-on bonus, benefits — not just the headline base salary.
- Research market range for the role, level, and location using multiple sources (public compensation-sharing communities, levels-based comparison sites) rather than a single anecdote.
- If you have a competing offer, that's your strongest leverage — mentioning it (without necessarily disclosing the exact number) is standard practice, not an aggressive move.

**How to actually counter:**
- Counter the total package, not just base salary — equity and sign-on bonus often have more room to move than base does.
- Anchor with a specific, researched number rather than a vague "can you do better?" — specificity signals you've done the homework and are negotiating in good faith. A reasonable starting point, absent better market data of your own, is roughly **10-15% above the initial offer**; adjust up or down once you have real comp-band research for the specific level and location.
- Negotiate through your recruiter, not the hiring manager, wherever a recruiter is involved — recruiters typically have real negotiation authority and are the intended channel for this conversation, and keeping money separate from the hiring manager protects the working relationship you're about to start.
- Negotiate over a call or in writing, whichever you're more comfortable with — there's no meaningful penalty either way as long as you're clear and specific.
- If leveling itself feels off (the offer undersells your actual experience), that's a legitimate thing to raise too, separate from comp — a bad level compounds every future comp conversation at that company.

## What not to do

Don't lie about a competing offer that doesn't exist — this occasionally gets checked, and even when it doesn't, it's a bad foundation for a new working relationship. Don't negotiate against yourself by naming a number lower than your actual target "to seem reasonable." And don't let negotiation anxiety push you into accepting immediately on the call — it's completely standard to say "thank you, I'd like a day or two to review this" before responding with a counter.

Interview prep gets most candidates' full attention; the follow-up and negotiation stage gets almost none, despite directly affecting the outcome you actually walk away with. Treat it with the same deliberateness as the interview itself.
