Most interview-prep resources are a flat pile of questions. FF is built differently — Learn, Practice, Interview Prep, and Roadmaps are four connected systems that feed into each other by design, and using them in the right order is worth more than grinding any one of them in isolation.

## The four systems, and what each one is actually for

**Learn** is where a concept gets built from the ground up — a written guide, a live simulator you can manipulate, a coding challenge, interview questions, and a real project, all for the same concept (e.g. Closures, or React Rendering & Reconciliation). This is where you go when a topic feels shaky, not when you already understand it and just want reps.

**Practice** is where you drill implementation speed once a concept is solid — timed challenges across JavaScript, React, CSS, TypeScript, and System Design, graded automatically. This is reps, not first-time learning.

**Interview Prep** (this section) is where you rehearse the actual interview format — short Q&A collections (FF 75, FF JavaScript, FF React, FF Next.js), long-form System Design guides, spaced-repetition review, and these Playbooks.

**Roadmaps** sequences all of the above into a guided path — useful once you know your target role and want a fixed order rather than deciding what to study next yourself.

## The order that actually works

A common mistake is starting in Interview Prep — reading Q&A answers for concepts you've never really used. It feels productive because you're "doing interview prep," but the retention is shallow: you're memorizing an answer instead of understanding the thing the answer describes. The more effective loop, concept by concept:

1. **Learn's Understand tab** — read the guide, get the mental model right
2. **Learn's Simulate tab** — manipulate the live simulator until the behavior feels obvious, not just readable
3. **Practice** — drill 2–3 timed challenges on that concept until implementation is fast, not just correct
4. **Interview Prep** — now read the Q&A entries for that concept; they'll click immediately because you already understand the mechanism, not just the words
5. **Build** — ship the concept's project brief if you have time; nothing cements understanding like building something real with it

Repeat per weak concept, not all at once across the whole catalog.

## How to actually pick what to study

Don't start from the full concept list — start from your own gaps. If you bombed a `this`-binding question in a past interview, or you're not sure you could explain event delegation from memory, that's your entry point, not "JavaScript Runtime, chapter 1." The [Learn index](/learn) groups every concept by category and difficulty, so you can jump straight to the weak spot instead of working through everything sequentially.

## A realistic weekly structure

If you have a fixed timeline before an interview, a workable split is: 2–3 days per week on Learn+Practice for your weakest 1–2 categories, 1–2 days on Interview Prep collections and this Playbook, and — starting about a week out — daily short Interview Prep review sessions (spaced repetition) to keep everything fresh rather than front-loaded and forgotten. See the [Frontend System Design Playbook](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-system-design-playbook) and the [Behavioural Interview Playbook](/interview-prep/playbook/behavioural-interview-playbook) for the two formats that benefit most from dedicated, separate prep time rather than falling out of concept-by-concept study.

The underlying principle: interview prep that's built on real understanding survives follow-up questions and live-coding pressure. Interview prep that's built on memorized answers falls apart the moment an interviewer asks "why" instead of "what."
