A surprising number of interviews go poorly for reasons that have nothing to do with technical skill: a broken screen-share, a laggy coding environment, showing up with no questions prepared, or being so sleep-deprived from last-minute cramming that recall falls apart under pressure. This is a short, deliberately mechanical checklist — the goal is to remove every avoidable failure mode before the interview even starts.

## The night before

- **Confirm the format.** Is it a live shared editor (CoderPad, HackerRank, a Google Doc), your own IDE via screen share, or a whiteboard tool? Each has different constraints — a shared editor with no autocomplete changes how much you should rely on muscle memory for syntax.
- **Test your setup.** Camera, mic, screen-share permissions, and — if it's a video call — that the specific video platform being used (Zoom, Meet, a proprietary tool) actually works on your machine. Do this the night before, not five minutes before the call.
- **Re-read the job description.** Note the specific stack and responsibilities mentioned — if it lists "design systems" or "performance optimization," expect the interview to lean that direction, and have a relevant story or two ready.
- **Prepare 2–3 questions for the interviewer.** Every round typically ends with "do you have any questions for me?" Having nothing prepared is a missed signal — good questions read as genuine engagement, not just politeness. Ask about team structure, how frontend decisions get made, or what a strong first 90 days looks like — avoid questions Google could answer in ten seconds.
- **Stop studying new material.** Cramming a new topic the night before adds anxiety without meaningfully improving recall. Review a summary of what you already know instead, and prioritize sleep — tired recall under pressure is measurably worse than well-rested recall of slightly less material.

## The morning of

- **Eat something and hydrate** — low blood sugar mid-interview is a solvable problem you shouldn't have to solve live.
- **Arrive (or log in) 5–10 minutes early**, not 30. Excessive early arrival can read as anxious, and joining a video call 20 minutes early is often just awkward for the interviewer's schedule too. 5–10 minutes is the right window.
- **Have water within reach.** Talking continuously for 45–60 minutes is more physically demanding than it sounds.
- **Close every unrelated tab and app.** A stray Slack notification or a messaging popup during a screen share is a small but real distraction, both for you and visibly for the interviewer.
- **Open a scratch note** with the questions you prepared for the interviewer, so you're not trying to recall them from memory at the very end when you're mentally tired.

## During the interview

- **State your understanding of the prompt back before coding**, even briefly — it catches misunderstandings early and costs almost nothing.
- **Keep a visible train of thought** — this isn't logistics so much as a reminder tied to [What Interviewers Actually Score](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-interview-playbook/what-interviewers-actually-score): silence is the most common and most fixable mistake in the room.
- **If technology fails** — a shared editor freezes, the call drops — stay calm and say so plainly ("looks like the call dropped, reconnecting now"). Interviewers have seen this before; a graceful recovery isn't held against you.

## After the interview

- **Send a short thank-you note within 24 hours** if you have the interviewer's contact info (not always available, and that's fine either way). Keep it brief — a genuine one-line note about something specific from the conversation beats a generic template.
- **Write down what you remember** — questions asked, what went well, what you'd do differently — while it's fresh. This is the single best source of material for calibrating prep before your next interview. See [Post-Interview Follow-Up and Negotiation](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-interview-playbook/post-interview-follow-up-and-negotiation) for what to do next.

None of this replaces technical preparation. But a strong technical performance undone by a broken microphone or a forgotten thank-you note is a genuinely common and entirely avoidable outcome — this checklist exists to make sure your prep time actually gets to show up on interview day.
