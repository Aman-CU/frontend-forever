Most candidates walk into their first frontend interview without knowing what the whole process actually looks like end to end. That gap causes real damage: people over-prepare for the wrong stage, panic when a recruiter call turns technical, or burn their best answers in a screen that was never going to be the bar-raising round. Knowing the shape of the process in advance is free preparation — it costs nothing and changes how you allocate the next several weeks.

## The typical timeline

A frontend interview process at a mid-size-to-large tech company usually takes 2–4 weeks from first contact to offer, broken into distinct stages:

1. **Recruiter screen** (20–30 min) — fit, logistics, comp expectations, not technical
2. **Technical phone screen** (45–60 min) — one coding or JavaScript-fundamentals round, usually over a shared editor
3. **Onsite / virtual onsite loop** (3–5 rounds, often same day) — coding, UI implementation, system design, and behavioral
4. **Hiring committee / debrief** — interviewers write feedback, a committee or the hiring manager makes the call
5. **Offer and negotiation**

Startups often compress this to 2–3 stages total; large companies (Google, Meta, Amazon-scale) tend to add a hiring committee step that adds a week or two of latency after your last interview, which is normal and not a bad sign.

## What each stage is actually testing

**The recruiter screen** is not neutral — recruiters do filter on comp mismatch, notice period, and basic communication clarity. Answer confidently, keep it under the time box, and never guess at a comp number you haven't researched.

**The technical phone screen** exists to filter out candidates who can't code under any pressure at all. It's usually the lowest bar in the whole loop. Interviewers are checking for: can you write correct, running code without a compiler catching your syntax errors for you, and can you talk while you do it. See [The 5 Interview Formats You'll Face](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-interview-playbook/the-five-interview-formats) for what "coding" actually splits into at the onsite stage.

**The onsite loop** is where the real signal gets collected. Each round is scored independently and mapped against a leveling rubric — a Senior-level candidate isn't just expected to get the right answer, they're expected to get there while explaining tradeoffs, catching their own edge cases, and communicating like someone who'll mentor others. [What Interviewers Actually Score](/interview-prep/playbook/frontend-interview-playbook/what-interviewers-actually-score) breaks this down round by round.

**The hiring committee** (where it exists) reviews written feedback from every interviewer, not just a majority vote. One strong "no hire" with specific, well-documented concerns can outweigh three lukewarm "hire"s — this is why a single bad round can sink an otherwise strong loop, and why panicking after one rough interview and coasting through the rest is the wrong response.

## Company-size differences worth planning around

| Company type | Typical loop length | What gets emphasized |
|---|---|---|
| Startup (under 200 eng) | 2–3 rounds, often 1 day | Shipping speed, full-stack range, culture fit |
| Mid-size scale-up | 3–4 rounds | Coding depth, UI craft, some system design |
| Big Tech (FAANG-scale) | 4–6 rounds + committee | Leveling rubric, system design at senior+, structured behavioral |

If you only have a few weeks, calibrate your prep to the company tier you're actually interviewing at rather than over-indexing on the hardest possible loop.

## What this means for how you prepare

Because the stages test different things, generic "practice more LeetCode" advice is incomplete. A realistic prep plan spreads time across formats in rough proportion to the loop: coding fundamentals, UI implementation under a timer, one system design framework you can repeat on any prompt, and a small bank of behavioral stories you can adapt on the fly. The single most common mistake at this stage isn't a skill gap — it's not knowing the process well enough to prepare for the right thing at the right time. Fix that first; it's the cheapest win available to you.
